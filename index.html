About Me

I'm an organismal biologist, currently doing a PhD in Ecology and Evolutionary Biology at Cornell University. I was born and raised in Ecuador, where I quickly developed a deep interest in the extraordinary biodiversity around me. I spent most of my undergraduate career chasing neotropical spiders, conducting fieldwork in tropical montane forests and the Amazon basin. More recently, I've turned to the natural compounds that mediate ecological interactions — questions I'm exploring throughout my graduate work.

I am committed to creating equal opportunity and representation in science, especially for those from developing countries. I believe diversity is an asset that strengthens our community through the enrichment of varied backgrounds, life experiences, and perspectives.

Research

My research interests lie at the intersection of chemical ecology and agroecology. By studying diversified cropping systems, I aim to understand the chemical and ecological mechanisms through which species interactions — both above and below ground — mediate resistance to herbivores. I also work in naturally evolving tropical systems to test hypotheses about the maintenance of phytochemical diversity in plants.

Intercropping and cover cropping differentially alter maize secondary metabolism and affect herbivore resistance

This study examines how intercropping and cover cropping practices reshape maize secondary metabolism and, in turn, plant resistance to herbivores. Using metabolomic profiling and bioassays, we identify shifts in defensive chemistry that depend on the identity of the companion plant and the timing of the diversification strategy.

Seed chemical treatments and their impact on the soil microbiome

We evaluate how commonly used seed chemical treatments — fungicides, insecticides, and biological coatings — influence the assembly and function of the rhizosphere microbiome, with downstream consequences for plant performance and belowground community dynamics.

Phytochemical diversity correlates with herbivore interactor diversity in the Andean shrub Baccharis latifolia

Working in Andean ecosystems, this project tests whether plant chemical diversity scales with the diversity of associated herbivores. We use field surveys and untargeted metabolomics in Baccharis latifolia to evaluate hypotheses on the maintenance of phytochemical diversity.

Read the publication →

Intercrop-mediated inducibility affects direct defenses and herbivore resistance, but not indirect defenses, in maize

This work shows that intercropping primes maize for stronger induced direct defenses and improved herbivore resistance, while indirect defenses (volatile-mediated natural enemy attraction) remain largely unchanged. The results have implications for designing biodiversity-based pest management.

How does intercropping facilitate beneficial maize–AMF interactions and induced responses?

An ongoing project investigating how intercropping with legumes shapes arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) communities associated with maize, and how those belowground partnerships modulate inducible defenses against aboveground herbivores.
